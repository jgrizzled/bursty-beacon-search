# The Bursty Beacon Search

## Plain English Summary

**What is this?** A statistical search for signs that any known repeating fast radio burst (FRB) source might be an artificial beacon rather than a natural object — carried out entirely on published, archival data, with the analysis locked down in advance so we can't fool ourselves.

**The idea: what would a rational beacon builder actually build?** The full argument is developed in the research note, [The Bursty Beacon Search](notes/The_Bursty_Beacon_Search.md); the short version follows.

A civilization that wants the largest number of *other* civilizations to find its beacon faces an economics problem, not just an engineering one. Broadcasting in all directions at once wastes almost all of the power on empty sky. The affordable design — familiar from the cost-optimized beacon literature — is a narrow, powerful beam swept across the sky like a lighthouse: each patch of sky gets illuminated for a fraction of a second, then the beam moves on and returns on a repeating schedule. The builder tunes range first, then how often the beam comes back, because a signal seen once and never confirmed is a signal forgotten.

The subtler question is *who the signal should be designed for*. The builder can't assume anyone runs dedicated SETI monitors, and small dishes pointed at one spot at a time have almost no chance of catching a brief beam sweep even once, let alone the two-plus times needed to believe it. What the builder *can* assume is that any civilization doing radio astronomy builds wide-field fast-transient survey instruments — because interesting natural phenomena (like FRBs) force them to — and that physics alone pins down what those instruments look for: millisecond-scale, dispersed radio bursts in roughly the 0.5–2 GHz window. So the rational discovery signal is engineered to *look like a natural radio burst*: natural-looking bursts pass through the observatories' automated detection pipelines, get saved to archives, and get published. The design principle in the note: **the outer envelope should get through the pipeline; the inner structure should establish artificiality.**

Where would the artificial giveaway hide? Not in what a single burst looks like — natural bursts are messy and varied enough to imitate almost anything once. It would be in the *timing*. A receiver only sees a burst when the rotating beam happens to point at it, so the arrival times would follow a strict hidden schedule: brief "visits" recurring at an exact period, possibly with each visit doubled — a quick confirmation pass minutes to hours after the first, so the recipient's alert is still active when the second burst lands. And because the beam's footprint grows with distance exactly as fast as the required power does, the per-recipient cost of this scheme is distance-invariant out to tens of megaparsecs: a sufficiently capitalized builder in a *nearby galaxy* would deliver events that are, observationally, genuine FRBs — real dispersion, real host galaxy — with artificiality resting entirely on the timing schedule.

The interesting consequence: if such beacons exist and have been caught by our telescopes, the evidence may already be sitting in public data — filed under "repeating FRB," with a name and a burst-statistics paper, but with a timing test that nobody has ever run (a gap confirmed by this project's [novelty audit](phase0/novelty_audit.md)). Published repeater catalogs record when every burst arrived and (crucially, for some campaigns) exactly when the telescopes were and weren't watching. That's everything needed to check.

**What we actually test.** For each source we fit six competing statistical models to the burst arrival times, always accounting for exactly when telescopes were observing (you can't see bursts while nobody is looking, and observing schedules are themselves streaky in ways that can masquerade as a signal). The models, grids, and decision rules are frozen in the [H1 pre-registration](phase0/prereg_h1.md):

- Four **natural** models: bursts arriving at random; a source with periodic active/quiet seasons (a known, natural behavior of some repeaters); bursts arriving in clumps and storms (also known and natural); and a source whose rate simply varies from session to session.
- Two **beacon** models: bursts confined to brief, strictly periodic "visits" (a sweeping beam), and the same with each visit doubled (the confirmation pass).

The headline number, **Λ (lambda)**, measures how much better the best beacon model explains the data than the best natural model. Positive means the beacon model fit better; that alone proves nothing, because a flexible model fit to streaky data through a gappy observing schedule can win by chance. So no result counts until **calibration** (specified in the [calibration plan](phase1/CALIBRATION_PLAN.md), implemented in [calibration.py](phase1/calibration.py) and run via the [cluster layer](cluster/README.md)): thousands of simulated datasets are generated from purely natural models, pushed through the identical search, and we count how often blind chance produces a score as large as the real one. Only if fewer than 1 in 1,000 simulations do so — and the signal doesn't sit at a period contaminated by the observing schedule itself (a daily rhythm, or the cadence with which the telescope revisited the source; the pre-computed contaminated-period lists live in [phase1/grids/](phase1/grids/)) — and no pulsar, interference, or instrumental issue can explain it — would anything be called a candidate.

To keep the test honest, the entire analysis — the models, the search grids, the thresholds, the burst lists ([data manifest](phase0/data_manifest.md)), the contaminated-period lists, the pinned software environment ([environment_pin.json](phase0/environment_pin.json)) — was frozen and tagged in git *before* any real data was scanned. The pipeline also had to pass validation first, including blind recovery of the two repeaters with published natural periodicities.

**What's in the sample.** Six observing campaigns on four repeating FRBs — the only published campaigns that include complete machine-readable observing logs, which most papers omit (selection criteria and the verification pass are in [prereg_h1.md](phase0/prereg_h1.md), Section 3): FRB 20201124A (FAST), FRB 20220912A (Nançay, and a four-telescope European network), FRB 20240114A (FAST, and the Tianma 65 m dish), and FRB 20200120E (Effelsberg). The last of these lives in a globular cluster of the galaxy M81, just 3.6 million light-years away — by far the nearest known repeater, which matters because it is the only source in the sample inside the beacon-economics horizon described above.

**Where things stand (August 2026).** The frozen confirmatory scan ([CONFIRMATORY_PLAN.md](phase1/CONFIRMATORY_PLAN.md), executed by [confirmatory.py](phase1/confirmatory.py)) is complete on all six campaigns; full numbers are in [confirmatory_results.json](phase1/confirmatory_results.json):

- In four campaigns, natural models win decisively. In a fifth (Tianma), the comparison is a statistical dead heat.
- The one standout is **FRB 20200120E**: the beacon models beat every natural model with a score of Λ = +86.78, the study-wide maximum. Its best-fitting period (~2.96 days) falls on a known schedule artifact (one third of a sidereal day) and is therefore disqualified, but several runner-up periods fit almost exactly as well and are clean of schedule artifacts. The mundane-explanation checks — catalogued pulsars, Galactic foreground, interference, instrument logs — all pass (see [contamination_check.md](phase1/contamination_check.md)).
- **This is not a detection claim, and we are not making one.** Most of this source's bursts arrived in a single two-hour storm, and a burst storm seen through sparse observing windows is exactly the kind of data that lets a "visit" model win for boring reasons — many unrelated periods fit equally well, which is the signature of that degeneracy, not of a beacon. Whether a score of 86.78 is even slightly unusual for a natural bursty source observed through these particular windows is precisely what the calibration simulations (currently being run) will determine. Our pre-registered expectation for the study is a null result, whose value is a quantitative constraint: it would tell us what kinds of beacon schedules are now ruled out for these sources.
